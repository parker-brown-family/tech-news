06 The claims as hypotheses, and what the counts say

Four of the post's claims written as hypotheses, each with a line that would support it and a line that would refute it. Each can reach every verdict, so none is unfalsifiable. They are post hoc: the counts existed before the lines were written, so this shows the claims fail tests they could have passed, not tests set in advance. Treat the verdicts as provisional.

lead · h-wave

Tarbell-linked writers drove the wave

Measured: 32 of 1,641 stories = 1.9%

refuted provisional · post hoc

Volume stands in for influence. A few pieces can set the tone for many, so a small share does not by itself rule out influence; the same count names the New York Times, which no source ties to Tarbell, as the tone-setter. The count of all stories is one analysis nobody has checked, and its window is not the same as ours.

rival · h-hits

The fellows wrote hit pieces

Measured: 3 of 23 argue = 13%

refuted provisional · post hoc

A headline is not the article: an explainer can lean, and a news story can be framed. This tests what the stories set out to be, not their tone.

ordinary · h-news

The fellows covered it as a news desk would

Measured: 20 of 23 = 87%

supported provisional · post hoc

Being supported does not show the coverage was fair, only that it was mostly ordinary news work in form.

rival · h-timing

The grant launch was timed to the story

Measured: 79% of days look as well timed

refuted provisional · post hoc

This shows the timing is no evidence. It cannot show nothing was planned; only a record of planning could show that, and none is public.

08 Where we left it: a confluence, not a campaign

Closed for now, 26 November 2026. The ties are real and the post is plausible on its face, but the simplest explanation is not a coordinated effort. It is a confluence: many outlets and writers under the same money and the same attention, moving the same way, which looks like coordination whenever a set of critical pieces arrives under one canopy. The name for it is emergent coordination, below. Whether the funding tilts the AI beat stays open.

09 Terms of the Art

The named ideas this check leans on, in plain words. The first is the one it closed on. They come from one list shared by all our news.

Emergent coordination

also: coordination without a coordinator, confluence

Many people acting on their own, reacting to the same events, incentives and to each other, move in the same direction, and the result looks planned although nobody planned it. The more attention and money sit over a subject, the stronger the pull in one direction, and the more it looks like a campaign.

Use it when a wave of similar stories, posts or moves is offered as proof that someone organised it. It does not mean it does not say nobody coordinated. It says coordination needs its own evidence, because the pattern alone is expected without it.

Spontaneous order (Wikipedia)

Stigmergy

Coordination through traces left in a shared space instead of through messages: each actor responds to what the last one left behind. A published story is such a trace; the next reporter, editor or poster reacts to it, so the stories line up without anyone talking.

Use it when explaining how an attention economy produces coordinated-looking output with no channel between the people involved. It does not mean not a metaphor for secret contact. The defining feature is that there is no direct contact.

Stigmergy (Wikipedia)

Pack journalism

Reporters at different outlets converge on the same story and the same angle, because they watch each other, share sources and fear missing what everyone else has. The name comes from Timothy Crouse's account of the 1972 US campaign press, The Boys on the Bus.

Use it when many outlets run near-identical framing within days of each other. It does not mean not proof the outlets agreed anything; the convergence is the ordinary behaviour of a press corps.

Pack journalism (Wikipedia)

Media feeding frenzy

also: pile-on

Once a public figure is caught in a scandal, coverage piles up fast and turns harsher, each story feeding the next. Larry Sabato named the pattern in his 1991 book Feeding Frenzy.

Use it when coverage of one person spikes after a single revelation, as the Huang coverage did after his admission on 8 November 2026. It does not mean it describes the dynamics of coverage, not whether the underlying story is true.

Media feeding frenzy (Wikipedia)

Structural bias

also: the propaganda model's filters

Who pays for coverage, and who gets hired to do it, can tilt what gets covered and how, with no instruction given. Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky's propaganda model makes this argument and says outright that it needs no conspiracy.

Use it when funding ties are real and the question is whether they shape coverage, rather than whether someone directed it. It does not mean not a finding that any given story was bent. It names a pressure to be measured, for example by comparing the tone of funded and unfunded coverage of the same events.

Propaganda model (Wikipedia)

Agent detection

also: hyperactive agency detection

People are quick to see a deliberate actor behind a pattern, because missing a real one used to cost more than imagining a false one. A wave of stories feels like someone's plan for the same reason.

Use it when asking why a coordinated reading is so persuasive before any evidence of coordination appears. It does not mean not an insult to whoever proposed the plot; it is how everyone's perception leans.

Agent detection (Wikipedia)

Occam's razor

Among explanations that fit the facts equally well, prefer the one that needs the fewest added assumptions. A campaign needs a coordinator, a channel and a plan that nobody has found; a confluence needs none of them.

Use it when two accounts both fit the record and one of them needs extra hidden parts. It does not mean a tiebreak, not a proof. New evidence of the hidden parts would overturn it.

Occam's razor (Wikipedia)

Base rate

How often something happens anyway, before any special explanation. A timing only means something if chance would rarely produce it: Tarbell announced something in the four weeks before 79 percent of days from July to late November 2026, so 'weeks before the story' was expected.

Use it when a coincidence of timing or membership is offered as evidence. It does not mean a high base rate does not show nothing happened; it shows the coincidence cannot be the proof.

Base rate fallacy (Wikipedia)

Competing hypotheses

also: analysis of competing hypotheses, ACH

Write several rival explanations before weighing anything, then judge evidence only by whether it tells them apart. Evidence that fits every rival equally decides nothing, however striking it looks. The method comes from Richards Heuer's work for intelligence analysts.

Use it when any contested claim, before reaching for the explanation that feels right. It does not mean not a vote counting rows in favour; the winner is the account that needs the fewest strained assumptions.

Analysis of competing hypotheses (Wikipedia)

Post hoc

A test whose pass-or-fail line was written after the data was already in hand. It shows how a claim fares, but not that it survived a test it could have failed fairly, because the line could have been placed to suit the answer.

Use it when reporting a verdict on lines set after counting. It does not mean not worthless; just weaker than a line agreed in advance, and it should say so.

Post hoc analysis (Wikipedia)
